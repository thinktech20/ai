BACKGROUND DISCUSSION
Prior to assembly of turbine wheels, the individual disks or wheels are balanced by the addition of weights installed in a
groove. Turbine wheels and spacers on 7F.05 and 7FA.04-200 units have a stakeless balance weight configuration (see
Figure 1); the retention of the weight attained by a set screw and staking on the wheel is not recommended. The turbine
blades are retained axially by a snap ring type lock wire located in a machined groove passing through the turbine blades
and turbine wheel. The portion of the wheel retaining the lock wire is known as the lock wire tab. The F-class turbine
blade cooling air supply is provided through cooling slots with the latest enhanced contour configuration in the bottom of
the stage 1 and 2 turbine wheel dovetails.
C
Figure 1: Stakeless Balance Weight
The disks in the turbine rotor are made of a high nickel alloy that is sensitive to surface imperfections where an
intergranular crack may initiate and propagate due to high stresses and temperatures. 7F.05 and 7FA.04-200 wheels are
peened from birth, which counteracts the effects resulting from surface imperfections. Depending on the initiation
location, an undetected crack may continue to propagate due to exposure to stress and temperature until either:
1- The crack is detected and either repaired or the rotor is removed from service, or
2- The crack propagates to a critical length, releasing a wheel section and/or adjacent turbine blades into the turbine hot
gas path section, potentially resulting in significant damage.
Recommended inspections and inspection intervals to help mitigate crack propagation are provided in Table 1 below.
This TIL is being issued for the 7F.05 and 7FA.04-200 turbine rotors to provide specific recommendations for this
configuration.
Lock wire Tabs
Lock wire tab (LWT) cracks have been detected on previous configurations of F-class turbine rotor wheels due to the
high stress in this region. These types of cracks can typically be repaired on site, to repair limits, without the need for
rotor disassembly or wheel replacement.

Visual (VT) and Borescope (BI)
It is a good practice for all users to evaluate the surface of their rotor, when available, by performing a visual inspection.
In order to complete a visual inspection, observe and borescope all of the accessible rotor surfaces. Specifically, look for
overall surface conditions such as dents, dings, nicks, scratches, crack-like indications, raised material, etc.
Key features to examine:
Broach slot fillets, slot bottoms, tangs, and cooling slots (Visual)
Turbine blade cooling slots in wheel broach slots (Visual/BI)
Spacer rim seal tooth rubs/coating (Visual)
Balance weights: looseness/migration, set screw staking quality, tight set screw (Visual/BI)
Nut damage or surface indications in flanges and webs (BI)
Counterbore outside diameter ligaments on distance piece (UT)
Fluorescent Penetrant Inspection
Fluorescent penetrant inspection (FPI) is recommended to detect cracks on lock wire tabs. However, it is not
recommended for the wheel posts, as cracks under the dovetail tangs are typically closed.
Recommended features to inspect:
Lock wire tab fillets: When turbine blades are removed during routine maintenance intervals.
TAS and DP with staked balance weights
TW1 Forward Web - see Figure 4
If crack-like indications are found, please contact your local GE Vernova service representative for disposition. In most
cases, an in-situ repair procedure can be used to remove cracked lock wire tabs or balance weight stake marks. A few
isolated instances have occurred where wheel replacement was necessary as the repair limits were exceeded.

Dovetail ECI should be performed until rotor retirement at the frequency defined in Table 1 and is available and
recommended for all wheel posts referenced. Third stage wheel posts and turbine wheels may also be inspected with the
same equipment, if elected. Prior to the ECI, the turbine rotor must be appropriately cleaned to facilitate proper
functioning of the ECI equipment and accurate readings. A preferred method for cleaning is by dry ice (CO₂) blasting.
This cleaning typically requires a single 12-hour shift to clean the rotor after turbine blade removal and requires the
surfaces be free of oil beforehand. If CO₂ blast is not available, a more arduous and lengthy hand-cleaning process is
acceptable (2-3 shifts). The rotor inspection then takes approximately three shifts (~32 hours) to complete Stages 1 and
2. Both the rotor cleaning (performed by qualified vendor) and the turbine rotor inspection can be scheduled through your
local GE Vernova representative.
SCOPE OF WORK
Qualified vendor to perform CO₂ blast cleaning of turbine rotor.
GE Vernova qualified personnel to perform rotor inspections.
Rotor inspection equipment is provided and applied by GE Vernova.
Turbine blades should be removed prior to beginning these inspections per hot gas path outage scope.
CO blast cleaning of turbine rotor can be completed in one shift (12 hours), including a prior hand wiping to remove oil
from the dovetail surfaces if necessary. ECI of first and second stages can be completed in three shifts or all three stages
in four shifts. Total time between turbine blade removal and installation should be four shifts (two days). These durations
are estimates only.
