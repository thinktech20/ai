BACKGROUND DISCUSSION
The function of the turbine rotor in a heavy-duty gas turbine engine includes carrying turbine blade loads, transmitting
torque to the compressor and generator for power generation, as well as supplying the turbine blades with cooling air.
Prior to assembly, the individual disks or wheels are balanced by the addition of weights installed in a groove. The
weights are held in place by deforming the metal on either the balance weight or the balance weight groove. The turbine
blades are retained axially by a snap ring type lock wire that is located in a machined groove passing through the turbine
blades and turbine wheel. The portion of the wheel retaining the lock wire is known as the lock wire tab. The F-class
turbine blade cooling air supply is provided through cooling slots in the bottom of the stage 1 and 2 turbine wheel
dovetails.

The disks in the turbine rotor are made of a high nickel alloy that is sensitive to surface imperfections where an
intergranular crack may initiate and propagate due to high stresses and temperatures. Depending on the initiation
location, an undetected crack may continue to propagate due to exposure to stress and temperature until either:
1- the crack is detected and either repaired or the rotor is removed from service, or
2- the crack propagates to a critical length, releasing a wheel section and/or adjacent turbine blades into the turbine hot
gas path section potentially resulting in significant damage.

Continuous improvements have been made to the turbine rotor wheels over time in order to improve their overall damage
sensitivity.
Rotors operating in high ambient and humid or corrosive conditions have an increased risk of crack initiation and
propagation. Recommended inspections and inspection intervals to help mitigate crack propagation are provided based
on wheel features, such as: shot peening condition at first fire, cooling slot geometry, material heat treatment, and
environmental conditions. In some cases, rotors may be swapped from site to site in order to expedite an outage. This
TIL is being issued to all general F-Class turbine rotors in order to provide recommendations for the various
configurations. Rotors operating in high ambient and/or corrosive conditions have an increased risk of crack initiation and
propagation and are addressed in TIL 1937.
Shot Peening Effect
Early generation turbine rotors have the highest sensitivity to surface imperfections. These rotors were manufactured and
fired without full shot peen coverage of the turbine wheels. Shot peening leaves a residual compressive stress and
decreases the risk of initiating a crack. Wheels that operated without shot peening have an increased risk of initiating and
propagating a crack in the rim region of the turbine rotor and in the turbine wheel dovetail slots. After 1996, all F-class
turbine wheels shipped with full peening coverage; these wheels were never fired without shot peening.
Cooling Slot
During operation, the intersection of the cooling slot and dovetail slot is subjected to high temperatures and tensile
stresses caused by turbine blade pull. The high temperatures and stresses in conjunction with an adverse edge
condition have been known to initiate cracks in this location.

Balance Weight Groove
Cracking has been observed on several rotors at the balance weight groove (BWG) during routine inspections (Figure
6). It has been found that this cracking initiated at an adverse edge condition because of the variability in the balance
weight retention method or at a location where the wheel surface was affected by an assembly or disassembly process
that may have locally altered the stress state. Cracks in the forward wheel web, if left unaddressed, could result in a
partial wheel material liberation, particularly when located on the stage 1 wheel.

Visual, Borescope (BI), and Ultrasonic (UT)
A visual inspection of all exposed turbine rotor surfaces is recommended for all non-shot peened rotors; however, it is a
good practice for all users to evaluate the surface of their rotor when available. In order to complete a visual inspection,
observe and borescope all of the accessible rotor surfaces. Specifically, look for overall surface conditions such as dents,
dings, nicks, scratches, crack-like indications, raised material, etc.
Key features to examine:
Broach slot fillets, slot bottoms, tangs, and cooling slots (Visual)
Turbine blade cooling slots in wheel broach slots (Visual/BI)
Spacer rim seal tooth rubs/coating (Visual)
Balance weights: looseness/migration, staking quality, tight set screw (Visual/BI)
Nut damage or surface indications in flanges and webs (BI)
Counterbore outside diameter ligaments on distance piece and turbine aft shaft (UT)
Fluorescent Penetrant Inspection
Fluorescent penetrant inspection (FPI) is recommended to detect cracks on balance weights and lockwire tabs. However,
it is not recommended for the wheel posts, as cracks under the dovetail tangs are typically closed.
Recommended features to inspect:
Staked balance weight grooves: at all major inspection intervals and when the CDC is removed during a HGPI.
Lock wire tab fillets: When turbine blades are removed during routine maintenance intervals.
If crack-like indications are found, please contact your local GE Vernova service representative for disposition. In most
cases, an in-situ repair procedure can be used to remove cracked lock wire tabs or balance weight stake marks. A few
isolated instances have occurred where wheel replacement was necessary as the repair limits were exceeded.

Dovetail ECI is recommended for all wheel posts until rotor retirement at the frequency defined in Tables 1-6. Third stage
wheel posts and turbine wheels with any cooling slot configuration may also be inspected with the same equipment, if

Prior to the ECI, the turbine rotor must be appropriately cleaned to facilitate proper functioning of the ECI equipment and
accurate readings. A preferred method for cleaning is by dry ice (CO₂) blasting. This cleaning typically requires a single
12-hour shift to clean the rotor after turbine blade removal, and also requires the surfaces be free of oil beforehand. In
the event that CO₂ blast is not available, a more arduous and lengthy hand-cleaning process is acceptable (2-3 shifts).
The rotor inspection then takes approximately three shifts (~32 hours) to complete Stages 1 and 2. Both the rotor
cleaning (performed by qualified vendor) and the turbine rotor inspection can be scheduled through your local GE
Vernova representative.
Modified Stage 1 and 2 Turbine Blades
The potential for crack initiation at the cooling slot location and lock wire tabs can be improved by reducing the amount of
turbine blade load passing through this area of the wheel. GE Vernova has developed a turbine blade modification to
change the load distribution in the wheel post, thereby decreasing the stress level at the cooling slot. This modification is
accomplished by machining a tapered relief into the turbine blade dovetail pressure faces adjacent to the cooling slot
(see Figure 9). This relief is often referred to as a dovetail "back cut". Relief cut modifications have been incorporated
into new production F-class turbine blades.

SCOPE OF WORK
Turbine blades should be removed prior to beginning these inspections per hot gas path outage scope. CO2 blast cleaning
of turbine rotor can be completed in one shift (12 hours), including a prior hand wiping to remove oil from the dovetail
surfaces if necessary. ECI of first and second stages can be completed in three shifts, or all three stages in four shifts. Total
time between turbine blade removal and installation should be four shifts (two days). These durations are estimates only.
